01

Understanding the Layout

Four Connected Keeps

Three smaller keeps stand around the main keep. Four roofed passage towers, called watari-yagura, connect them around a courtyard.

Not one keep, but four connected buildings

The main keep commands attention from the front, but the Inui, East and West keeps stand around it. The I, Ro, Ha and Ni passage towers connect them. The term renritsu-shiki, a linked group of keeps, becomes easier to understand when you follow the roofs.

Positions, roof tiers and floors+ Read more− Close
Main Keep Southeast
5 exterior roof tiers / 6 floors above ground and 1 basement level
Inui Keep Northwest
3 exterior roof tiers / 4 floors above ground and 1 basement level
East Keep Northeast
3 exterior roof tiers / 3 floors above ground and 1 basement level
West Keep Southwest
3 exterior roof tiers / 3 floors above ground and 2 basement levels

The number of exterior roof tiers does not necessarily match the number of interior floors. Floor counts follow the City of Himeji designated cultural properties list.

When photographing, look at the layers of roofs

From Otokoyama, the smaller keeps overlap in front of the main keep. Before framing only the largest tower, try leaving the surrounding roofs in the picture. They reveal the castle as a group of buildings, not a solitary tower.

Once you know the layout, the order and overlap of the keeps in a photograph can help you work out which side of the castle it was taken from.

My favourite is the view from Otokoyama Reservoir Park. The Inui Keep, the largest of the three smaller keeps, stands at the centre in front, with the East Keep to the left, the West Keep to the right, and the Main Keep behind. To me, the depth created by those four overlapping buildings is Himeji Castle at its most dignified.

Sources: Himeji Castle: Enjoying the Castle (Japanese); City of Himeji: designated cultural properties (Japanese PDF); Official Himeji Castle guide, pp.43–46 (Japanese PDF)

02

Inside the Keep

Two Great Pillars

One to the east, one to the west. The two 24.6-metre pillars rise from the basement to just beneath the sixth floor, the topmost floor of the keep.

Look beyond the pillars to the connecting timbers

Both pillars connect to horizontal timbers at each floor. Look at how these members fit together as well as at the massive posts themselves, and the timber framework hidden behind the castle’s exterior begins to emerge.

The west pillar joint and four structural groups+ Read more− Close

The west pillar consists of two timbers joined at the third-floor level. The keep can also be understood as four structural groups: basement to second floor, third floor, fourth floor, and fifth to sixth floors. The great pillars and horizontal timbers connect these groups.

The diagram marks the location of the joint, not its actual joinery profile.

On Site / Before the Paid Entrance

See the old west pillar before entering the keep

In Sannomaru, before the paid entrance, the old west pillar that once supported the keep is on display. It served for roughly 350 years before being replaced during the major Showa restoration. It is not the pillar standing inside today, but seeing that long timber lying before you conveys a scale that is difficult to grasp inside the building.

An upper and a lower timber make one pillar

The old west pillar joined a lower section of fir (momi) to an upper section of hemlock (tsuga), above the third-floor boards. Compare the display labels 上柱 (upper pillar) and 下柱 (lower pillar): what appeared to be one great post was made from two trees.

The surfaces also bear cuts for connections to other members and traces of later repairs. Not every visible notch belongs to the joint between the upper and lower timbers.

East and west: different reasons for joining

West pillar
Originally made from an upper and lower timber. Replaced with new hinoki cypress during the Showa restoration, it still consists of two sections joined at the third-floor level.
East pillar
Originally one unjointed fir timber. During the Showa restoration, its bottom 5.4 metres were replaced with Taiwan cypress and spliced to the retained original timber above.

“Two timbers in the west, one in the east” does not fully describe the pillars today. The east pillar also has a splice where its damaged base was replaced. Their differences tell a story of both original construction and the repairs needed to preserve it.

The new west pillar was meant to be one timber+ Read more− Close

During the major Showa restoration (1956–1964), decay was found inside the old west pillar and replacement became necessary. The plan was to use a single long hinoki cypress timber.

But the selected log broke while being hauled out of the mountains. Two timbers were therefore joined at the third-floor level, as before. The present west pillar still bears witness to the challenges faced by the restoration team.

Sources: Japan Tourism Agency: The Old West Pillar (English). Photographs of the joint during the restoration are also available in Himeji Castle’s Showa restoration photographs: great pillars (Japanese).

The display sign and the old pillar’s dimensions

The sign lists an upper timber of 12.4 metres, a lower timber of 14.5 metres and an overlap of 2.2 metres at the joint. Subtracting that overlap from the combined lengths gives a total of 24.7 metres.

The 24.7-metre figure comes from the sign for the old pillar on display. The structural diagram above uses 24.6 metres, as given in the official guide’s description of the great pillars.

From the display to the inside of the keep

Climb the stairs after seeing the length of the old timber, and the pillars and beams take on a different meaning. On the top floor, remember the great pillars reaching to just below your feet. The structure that lets you stand there can be as rewarding to notice as the view outside.

Sources: On-site Old West Pillar sign (photographed 17 March 2026); Japan Tourism Agency: The Old West Pillar (English); Official guide, p.47: continuous-pillar construction (Japanese PDF); Hyogo Prefectural Museum of History: Himeji Castle (Japanese). The display location is as observed on the photography date. For current access, consult the official Himeji Castle website (Japanese) and on-site signs.

03

Reading the Roof Details

Gables and Ornaments

Hafu are the gables that shape the roofline; gegyo are the pendants beneath them. Compare the triangles, curves and small ornaments at their tips to discover the character of these overlapping roofs.

Three kinds of gable

Irimoya-hafu 入母屋破風
The triangular gable at the end of a hip-and-gable roof. Its connection to the main roof ridge distinguishes it from a chidori-hafu.
Chidori-hafu 千鳥破風
A decorative, subsidiary gable projecting from partway down a roof. It gives the main roof an accent; examples with windows also admit daylight.
Noki-karahafu 軒唐破風
A gently curved karahafu gable incorporated into the eaves. Its flowing outline contrasts with the triangular gables.

Gegyo: pendants beneath the gable

A gegyo hangs at the central tip of a gable and conceals the end of the ridge timber. Look for these three forms at Himeji Castle.

Kabura-gegyo 蕪懸魚
A form widely used on the keeps. Notice the outline hanging down from the centre.
Mitsubana-kabura-gegyo 三花蕪懸魚
A combination of three kabura forms, producing a more elaborate silhouette that spreads to both sides.
Umebachi-gegyo 梅鉢懸魚
A pentagonal pendant often used on chidori gables.
Unokedoshi and kaerumata: compare their positions+ Read more− Close

兎毛通 (unokedoshi) is a horizontally extended pendant fitted to a curved karahafu gable. 蟇股 (kaerumata), shown in green in the diagram, is a different member on the small wall below the curved eaves. Position, as well as shape, helps distinguish them.

When photographing, move from outline to detail

Begin with the triangles and curves of the roofline, then look at the small ornaments below. Whether you frame the whole keep or concentrate on its roofs, there is pleasure in finding the differences between these forms.

Source: Himeji City Castle Research Center: Basic Knowledge of Himeji Castle (Japanese)

04

Understanding the white exterior

White Plaster and Roof Joints

Himeji Castle's whiteness does not stop at its walls. It extends beneath the eaves, over window lattices and between dark roof tiles. Follow those white lines to discover the craft that protects a timber castle.

Wrapping a timber castle, from walls to eaves

In the finish known as shiro-shikkui so-nurigome-zukuri, white lime plaster covers not only the earthen walls concealing the columns but also details beneath the eaves, rafters, window lattices, gables and pendant ornaments. It gives the castle its white appearance while helping protect its timber from fire.

Timber posts and beams support the building. A lattice of branches or other laths holds the wall earth, which is finished with white plaster. The cutaway distinguishes the load-bearing frame, the lath, the earthen wall and the surface finish.

White lines between dark tiles

Himeji Castle's hongawara-buki roof combines pan and cover tiles. Lime mortar applied at joints, including those between cover tiles, helps protect against rain and wind. The contrast between dark tiles and white joints also contributes to the castle's white appearance.

Pan tiles hiragawara
Shallow, concave tiles laid with the hollow facing upwards to carry rainwater down the roof slope.
Cover tiles marugawara
Semicylindrical tiles placed over the joints between neighbouring rows of pan tiles.

The raised lines on a roof can make the two hard to distinguish. Look at their cross-sections: a pan tile forms a channel for water; a cover tile caps a joint.

Plaster thickness and the craft of repair+ Read more− Close

The plaster on the main keep's exterior walls is described as approximately 30 mm thick. This is not a uniform specification for the entire castle: thickness and composition vary by location.

The white surface has not survived untouched for centuries. Craftspeople have renewed it through successive conservation campaigns, including the major Showa-era repairs and the Heisei-era conservation work. Traditional materials and plastering skills sustain the appearance we see today.

When photographing, follow the white

After photographing the whole keep, look a little closer: beneath the eaves, at a window lattice or gable, and along the white lines and shadows between tiles. What looked like a single white surface from a distance begins to reveal the work of individual craftspeople.

Sources (Japanese): Himeji City Castle Research Center: Basic Knowledge of Himeji Castle; City of Himeji: Castle Site Preservation and Utilization Plan (PDF); Himeji Convention & Visitors Bureau: castle visitor guide; Kajima: Himeji Castle conservation work; Japanese Association for Conservation of Architectural Monuments: traditional techniques

05

Beneath the castle

Stone Walls

Stone walls support buildings and terraces. Look at the surfaces and joints to see traces of construction and repair.

Start with the contact between stones

Nozura-zumi
Mostly undressed stones retain their natural shapes. Smaller stones also fill the gaps.
Uchikomi-hagi
Contact areas are dressed to reduce gaps, while the exposed surfaces retain some irregularity.
Kirikomi-hagi
Carefully dressed joints fit closely together. Examples survive in parts of the Sannomaru.

Look at the corners and the curve

Sangi-zumi alternates the direction of long stones at a corner. It describes corner construction, rather than the degree of stone dressing. The west side of Bizenmaru also shows the rising curve known as the “fan slope.”

Stone size alone cannot date a wall. Rebuilding and later additions also shape what you see.

Photograph the base as well as the keep

Include the stonework beneath the white towers. Its texture gives weight and scale to the rooflines above.

Sources: Japan Tourism Agency, Stone Walls (Japanese PDF); Himeji City Castle Research Office, Layout and Earthworks; Hyogo Prefectural Museum of History, Himeji Castle

06

How the castle was defended

Loopholes (Sama)

Circles, triangles and rectangles punctuate the white walls. These openings served archers and gunners; their depth matters as much as their outline.

Tall slits, circles, triangles and squares

Arrow slits — yazama
Tall rectangular slits allowed archers to shoot through the wall.
Gun loopholes — teppozama
Circular, triangular and square openings are among the forms found in the walls.

Small outside, wider inside

A loophole generally narrows toward the outside face. This gives a broad view from within while making the interior less visible from outside.

Some loopholes in the keep complex are now plastered over on the outside. Shallow recesses in the white wall can reveal their positions.

Follow the rhythm of the openings

After a close view, photograph the whole wall. Repeated openings and receding steps can lead the eye toward the keep.

Sources: Hyogo Prefectural Museum of History, Himeji Castle; Himeji Castle Official Guide, Loopholes and Stone Drops (Japanese PDF)
